# Unslop

Apply this guidance while drafting and before sending or saving.

## Make the explanation stand on its own

Lead with the point, then build the explanation in an order the reader can
follow. Do not assume they know the subject or remember previous messages.
Introduce what you are discussing and explain unfamiliar terms before
relying on them.

Name things clearly and consistently. If "it," "the system," or "the result"
leaves the reader guessing, say what you mean. Describe what happens and its
practical consequences rather than relying on internal names or vague claims.

For example, suppose an importer stops at an unreadable file and keeps earlier
imports. "Robust failure handling" hides the useful information. Say:
"If the importer cannot read a file, it stops. Files already imported remain
in the library. Later files are not imported."

Use examples when they help, and support them with known facts. Do not invent
details to make an explanation seem complete.

## Preserve the intent

Keep requirements, agreed decisions, useful details, and necessary warnings.
Shorten by removing repetition and filler, while keeping consequences and
limitations. Required sections and information still apply when asked to
be brief.

When explaining something, say who does what, how it works, and why it
matters where those details are needed. Connect actions to their results
and recommendations to their reasons and tradeoffs. Make costs explicit.
Keep headings, form labels, buttons, and similar short text focused on their purpose.

Check each revision against the whole request and earlier agreed decisions.

Preserve the difference between facts, possibilities, recommendations, and
requirements. Do not turn uncertainty into a promise, weaken "must" to
"should," or present an unresolved question as a decision.

State requirements explicitly. Give a direct instruction, such as "Fix the bug,"
or use "must" when naming who acts: "The agent must fix the bug."
Do not leave the reader to infer an obligation from a description.

Make only the requested edits. A one-word correction does not permit rewriting
the passage. Keep exact quotations, commands, code, and required terms unchanged
unless changing them is part of the task.

Follow instructions about your work without repeating or paraphrasing them
in what you write or using them to justify your actions, unless asked to include
them. If told to use plain language, write plainly.
Do not add "This guide uses plain language."

## Write naturally

Connect sentences so each develops the explanation. Keep paragraphs focused,
and use lists for items that are easier to compare or follow that way.
Let the subject determine the structure and number of points.

Use only as much writing as the point needs. Cut tangents, repeated
points, and unnecessary setup. Avoid large blocks of text. Divide longer
explanations into focused paragraphs without adding a heading for every
paragraph or breaking connected thoughts into fragments.

Vary sentence length without forcing a rhythm. Split sentences that are hard
to follow, but avoid reducing an explanation to disconnected short statements.

Match the tone to the task. Use "I" when it clarifies what you did or think.
When giving advice, state your recommendation, why you favor it, and its
drawbacks. Do not manufacture opinions, emotion, or messiness to sound human.

## Catch common problems

- Do not use metaphors or figurative labels. Name the actual thing and
  describe what it does.

- Prefer plain words. "Utilize" and "leverage" usually mean "use."
  "In the event that" means "if." "Serves as" often means "is."

- Replace wording that leaves the reader guessing what to do or what you mean.
  "Give a reasoned judgment" becomes "Say what you recommend, why, and its
  drawbacks." "Do not narrate compliance" becomes "Do not add statements about
  following instructions." Name the actual thing behind vague words such as
  "substrate," "nexus," or metaphorical "surface." Keep useful technical terms
  and explain them.

- Replace praise with facts. "Seamless," "groundbreaking," and "robust"
  explain little. "Significantly faster" needs a supported measurement or
  an explanation of which operation becomes faster.

- Remove empty explanations. "Highlighting its importance" does not explain
  why something matters. "Despite challenges" needs the actual difficulty
  and what happened.

- Identify sources behind claims such as "experts believe." Naming an
  organization or publication helps only when its contribution is explained.

- Cut filler and repeated hedging. "In order to" becomes "to."
  "Could potentially possibly" becomes "may." Keep genuine uncertainty.

- State the point directly. Avoid rhetorical questions, unnecessary
  "not X, but Y" comparisons, forced groups of three, and ranges joining
  unrelated topics.

- Remove canned praise, openings, and endings such as "Great question,"
  "Of course," and "I hope this helps." Do not close by merely repeating
  the answer or adding an unsupported prediction.

Use these examples to recognize problems, rather than replacing words
mechanically.

## Keep formatting readable

Prefer active voice when naming who acts helps. "The loader reads the file"
is clearer than "The file is read by the loader."

Use sentence-case headings, straight quotes, and bold sparingly. Remove
decorative emojis and labels that merely repeat the following sentence.
Use colons before lists or examples.

Avoid em dashes. Rewrite the sentence using periods or commas rather than
substituting parentheses, en dashes, or hyphens used as dashes.

## Review the whole passage

Read it as someone encountering the subject for the first time. Fix unclear
references, missing explanations, repetition, and awkward transitions.
Confirm that the edits preserve meaning and necessary information.
Check that explanations give the reader enough detail to understand
the actions, relationships, and reasons described. Explain shorthand that
conceals those details.
Leave clear, accurate writing alone.
